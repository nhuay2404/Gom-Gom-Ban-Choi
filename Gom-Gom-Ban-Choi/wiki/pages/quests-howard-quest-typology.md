# Quest typology: fetch, delivery, kill, escort, dungeon crawl

Source: [[quests-howard-src]], Ch. 5 "The Challenges of the Quest", pp. 101-105; also pp. 24-25, 123.

## Classification basis
Player communities and strategy guides (and Kelly for MMOs) use informal types; Howard analyses *Oblivion*'s: **fetch**, **delivery**, **dungeon crawl**, **escort**, **kill**. They can be subdivided, combined and layered with narrative back stories. A fuller typology would also record the objective, required actions and location; Aarseth proposed a "grammar" of quests (p. 101).

## Types
- **Fetch**: find a valuable (often magical) object and return it to an NPC for reward. Often derided as cliche, but the Holy Grail quest is a fetch quest; interest depends on the object's value/meaning and the obstacles. Range from "mundane but charming collection quests" (twelve slaughterfish scales) to cosmic (Great Sigil Stone) (pp. 101-102). Four middle *Oblivion* main quests are fetches of key artifacts.
- **Delivery ("fedex")**: carry an item from one character to another (p. 102).
- **Kill / assassination**: slay monsters or hostile NPCs, either an end (remedy an injustice) or a means (to reach a fetch item). The least meaningful form: "slay N monsters and bring back trophies"; more meaningful when the monster guards access (Campbell's *threshold guardian*; bosses) and when purpose determines consequence (killing citizens vs a demon lord yields jail vs reward) (pp. 102-104). Howard defends violence in kill quests as inherited from epic and romance.
- **Escort**: guide an NPC while protecting them (*Oblivion*: Martin from Kvatch) (p. 104).
- **Dungeon crawl**: defined by location (labyrinth with monsters and treasure); usually combined with kill and fetch, e.g. slay five undead in five areas to recover armor (p. 104).
- Others seen in *Oblivion*: detective investigations, vampire tales, political intrigue side quests (p. 108); activating a number of placeable objects (altars, statues) with a counter (pp. 157-159).

## Two kinds of challenge
**Combat** and **cognition** (puzzles), forced by effort ("ergodic") rather than observed (pp. 103-104; 117). Failure yields no progress or a negative outcome (death) (p. 116).

## Meaning ladder within the same type
The same fetch/kill can sit at different meaning levels depending on stakes, backstory and consequence (the Mage's Guild example, pp. 24-25). "Meaning" can also come from refusing: a kill quest may be solved by diplomacy or fleeing, which a standard plot template cannot register, so use custom variables (pp. 137-138).

## Stance and applicability
(a) RPG/adventure/MMO tasks with combat, travel and NPCs. (b) Practical taxonomy from community usage and one flagship game; no frequency or retention data. (c) Compare with the daily/weekly mission taxonomy in [[concept-spreadsheet-modeling]] (clear N pieces, easy/medium/hard) and the event-type catalogue in [[liveops-vasiuk-event-types-and-calendar]]: Howard's types are verbs of *world interaction*; mobile tasks are verbs of *core-loop counters* (match N, clear M levels, use a booster). The disdain for "kill N trophies" matches the criticism of grind in [[concept-dark-game-design-patterns]]. (d) Fit for casual match-3 + deco: **medium**. Usable ideas: (i) classify every task as "do X N times" (kill/collect analog) vs "reach a state" (fetch analog) vs "use a feature" (escort/delivery analog) when auditing variety; (ii) milestone Quests should avoid pure "N trophies" and, per the Mage's Guild lesson, vary framing and consequence while keeping the same underlying counters; (iii) allow more than one way to satisfy a goal. Daily tasks can stay plain counters.
