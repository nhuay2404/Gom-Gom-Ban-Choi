# Quest definition: quest vs quest game vs quest narrative

Source: [[quests-howard-src]], Preface and Introduction, pp. ix-6, 19-22.

## Definition and vocabulary
- **Quest** (Preface p. ix): a journey across a symbolic, fantastic landscape where a protagonist/player collects objects and talks to characters to overcome challenges and reach a meaningful goal. Latin *quaerere*, "to seek": a goal-oriented search for something of value (p. 3).
- Three related terms drawn as overlapping circles (Fig. 1, p. 2): **quest narratives** (epic/romance structure: Odyssey, Parzival, Faerie Queene), **quest games** (adventure, RPG, action-adventure, MMO), and **quests** in the overlap, the structure/activity that exists in both.
- Aarseth's quest definition: a player-avatar must move through a landscape to fulfil a goal while mastering a series of challenges (p. 2). Howard adds *meaning*.
- Quest vs journey: a journey is the spatial movement and duration a quest entails (p. 7).
- Quest vs adventure vs romance (Frye): romance = genre; adventure = its sequential content; quest = the big central adventure that gives form (p. 8).
- Word choice matters: "quest" for fantasy/mythic settings, "mission" for urban/ninja/crime settings; quest implies higher stakes than stealing a car for a gang (p. 23). A lost button or Pac-Man dots is not a quest (p. 1).

## Enactment, not interactivity
Juul's point (p. 2): quests contain predefined sequences of events that the player must *actualize*. Howard prefers **enactment**: interactivity is a prerequisite but not sufficient; enactment means overcoming specific challenges that produce particular events, usually balancing long- and short-term goals. A reader watches the hero succeed regardless; a player must exert effort or the events never happen (pp. 2, 111).

## Ludology vs narratology
Narratologists see games as stories; ludologists see rules/simulation. Howard says the debate is winding down; quests are one bridge (pp. 1-4). Related claims:
- Aarseth's *interpretative* (reading) vs *configurative* (playing) functions; Bogost's "unit operations"; Howard says quests are "progressive" and "systematic" where units are not (pp. 4-5).
- Tronstad: a quest withholds meaning (meaning kills the quest); Howard rejects this as a false binary, because thematic meaning can be re-enacted on replay (pp. 19-20).
- **Quest system** = the engine/interface part that tracks quests: one **main quest** plus many **side quests** (p. 5).
- Bateman's audience research: "quest" is one of three marketable game families (quest/strategy/simulation); buyers of quest games expect many hours and a wide epic scope (p. 3). The *Neverwinter Nights 2* tagline ("everything you do has a meaning") is cited as deliberate marketing to that audience.

## Stance and applicability
(a) Assumes RPG/adventure/interactive-narrative media with a protagonist, a world to traverse and a story at stake; casual mobile is never discussed. (b) Theory and literary/academic argument; no player data. (c) Qualifies, rather than conflicts with, [[concept-core-loop-and-metagame]]: Howard's "quest" is a story-bearing unit, whereas our Daily tasks and milestone quests are *metagame goal trackers* (cf. the mission example in [[concept-spreadsheet-modeling]]). His point that a task with no stakes is not really a "quest" is a naming warning: our daily tasks are missions/chores and should be framed as such. (d) Fit for a casual match-3 + deco game: **low**. The only transferable takeaway is that goals feel better when something visible is at stake (e.g. deco progress) rather than a bare counter; the theory itself does not inform Daily or milestone design.
