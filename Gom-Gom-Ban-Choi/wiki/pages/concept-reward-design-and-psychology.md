# Reward design and psychology

Sources: [[src-czerkawski-game-economy-design]] ch. 2 (pp. 40–49), ch. 4 (p. 105, 115–117).

Design levers
- **Fungibility**: currencies are fungible, high scores/unique items are not. Use non-fungible resources (Marvel Snap Boosters, card-specific) to make progress predictable and simulation easier; fungible Credits give player choice. Never pay a high score "in currency".
- **Divisibility**: give the main soft currency a large base (e.g. 1000 units) so rewards can be scaled up/down finely; a small base can be deliberate for a rare end-game currency.
- **Deterministic + non-deterministic rewards**: deterministic (match/daily rewards) builds habit and lets players plan; non-deterministic (rare drops) gives surprise and room for optimisation. Players save deterministic income to chase random rewards. Control early, release control later so top players can find optimal paths.
- **Frustration and failure**: the economy must allow failure; some things unattainable for all but the dedicated.
- **Keep it simple**: document the simulation, explain it to a colleague; if you can't, it's too complex. Spreadsheets don't show fun; meta systems can't be playtested in minutes, so use debug tools for fast progression.
- **Endowment effect**: people value what they own; a small chance of an above-tier item in a chest (Rare chest dropping an Epic) boosts attachment. Needs negative surprises too.
- **Peak-End rule**: people remember the peak and the end; give a high-note reward at the end of a session (e.g. streak reward) and a strong finale. Negativity bias outweighs positives.
- **Loss aversion** (monetization use): taking rewards away (RPG boss defeat, revive potion) pushes spending; see [[concept-monetization-strategies]].
- **Narrative framing** makes the economy itself fun (black market); work with narrative designers.
- Pricing biases (anchoring, decoy, compromise, Weber's law): [[concept-store-and-pricing]].
