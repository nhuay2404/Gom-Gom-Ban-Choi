# Rules: arbitrary relationships that give motion and objects meaning

Source: [[gamefeel-swink-src]], Ch. 5 (pp. 98-100), Ch. 11 (pp. 179-186), Ch. 13-16 rules sections, Ch. 19 (pp. 342-343).

## Claims
- A rule is an arbitrary, designed relationship between parameters or objects (100 coins = 1 star; 3 hits to kill; 5 stars open a door). Outside the system it means nothing; inside it creates value and therefore changes **how it feels to collect or avoid** (p. 179).
- Three levels: **high** (goals, reward hierarchy, lives/health; breadcrumbs that focus the player on a subset of motions), **mid** (objects with immediate temporary meaning: health pack at low health, flag carrier, power-ups; the world feels slower or more urgent), **low** (toughness: number of hits tells mass; heavier moves do more damage) (pp. 179-183).
- High-level reward makes low-level skill worth practising: Mario 64's coins connect to stars, doors and areas so precise steering matters; Ratchet and Clank's flood of bolts becomes "vacuuming"; Zelda grass is only fun because cutting it drops rupees; Diablo's ~1 drop per 100 kills carries tedious clicking (pp. 180-182).
- Rule scale gives perceived weight: Street Fighter II's best-of-three raises stakes after round 1 (focus on tiny motions); Cave Story's weapon-energy loss when hit forces weapon switching and keeps the movement meaningful; hit-counts form a "hierarchy of power" (Goomba, turtle, winged turtle, Bowser; Halo grunts vs brutes) (pp. 183-186, 226-227, 243-244, 274).
- Mario 64: coins heal, hazards cost more or fewer health points (lava 2, Goomba 1), bosses always need three hits ("rule of threes"), no time limit gives a sense of free discovery (pp. 274-275).
- Raptor Safari's philosophy: **reward every possible interaction** (impossible to score zero, "A for Effort" 50 points), special bonuses for every combination, combo chains linked across systems with bonus time, scoring scale learned by the player; achievements and leaderboards give long-term goals (pp. 294-296).
- Future: rules as an expressive medium (Passage, The Marriage), spatial relationships as meaning; Dawn of Sorrow's soul-drop-per-enemy rule turns every new enemy into a potential power-up (pp. 342-343).

## Stance and applicability
- (a) Arcade/console action; rules mostly lives, health, damage, score; no economy, no F2P.
- (b) Design analysis with case studies; rule effects on feel are asserted, not measured.
- (c) Overlaps [[concept-reward-design-and-psychology]] and [[concept-core-loop-and-metagame]] (value created inside the loop); for sinks and drops see [[concept-economy-building-blocks]]. Note the tension with [[src-zagal-dark-patterns-in-game-design]]: "reward everything" and Diablo-style drop-rate compulsion are exactly what that source labels risky when monetised; here they are offered as feel tools in a premium game. Casual/cozy limits: [[cozydive-bycer-low-stakes-design]].
- (d) Fit for a mobile casual match-3 + deco game: **medium**. The idea that value assigned by rules makes a small action (clear a tile, nudge a cat) feel worth doing and that toughness-by-hit-count reads as weight is useful for obstacles/blockers; goal hierarchy ties board moves to deco progress. Lives, damage and combat rules are not applicable.
