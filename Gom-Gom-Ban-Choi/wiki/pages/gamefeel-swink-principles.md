# The seven principles of game feel (plus ownership)

Source: [[gamefeel-swink-src]], Ch. 17 (pp. 297-309); parallels the principles of animation.

1. **Predictable results**: same input, expected response; hard to complex is fine, random is not. Three pitfalls: *control ambiguity* (simultaneous inputs resolved by milliseconds the player cannot control), *state overwhelm* (too many states with unclear transitions make input feel random; novices mash), *staging* (the result of an input must be perceivable: exaggerate with particles, one idea at a time, Lasseter). The first minutes decide retention; the player should infer the whole game structure from early interactions (pp. 298-300).
2. **Instantaneous response**: some obvious result within about 70-100 ms; the attack can be long if the first reaction is immediate (Halo Warthog); relates to slow-in/slow-out (pp. 300-301).
3. **Easy but deep**: natural mappings and standard conventions for ease; helpers (auto-aim, DDA, rubber-banding); depth through sensitivity, rules, context, timed records, competition and leaderboards (pp. 302-303).
4. **Novelty**: avoid repeating identical linear animation (Jak's punch gets stale); remedies: more content/context, new mechanics over time, physics systems that never repeat, subtle variation (nothing real repeats exactly) (pp. 303-304).
5. **Appealing response**: the control should be fun to play with in an empty space; add baked animation and effects for appeal; spend extra care on failure states, which players see most (Ski Stunt Simulator ragdoll crashes are fun enough that players crash on purpose) (pp. 304-305).
6. **Organic motion**: curved arcs beat linear; set velocity through forces rather than overwriting position (pp. 305-306).
7. **Harmony**: all six elements support one perceived reality; consistency of abstraction; motion is the hardest; small inconsistencies erode the world (pp. 306-308).
- **Ownership**: a sensitive, flexible mechanic enables improvisation, flow and self-expression; replays and styles (Tony Hawk lines, Ski Stunt) make players identify with and evangelise the game; in multiplayer, expression becomes communication (pp. 308-309).

## Stance and applicability
- (a) Real-time avatar games; "easy but deep" and ownership assume hundreds of hours of play and mastery.
- (b) Opinionated synthesis, with no data on which principle matters most; author admits the list is "not complete".
- (c) Compatible with [[cozydive-bycer-ui-ux-and-approachability]] (predictable, low friction) and [[mobiledesign-scolastici-four-keys-to-fun]]. Partial tension with casual/cozy design: ownership by mastery vs the relaxing curve in [[cozydive-bycer-relaxing-progression-curve]]; in a deco game the ownership route is personalisation, which [[cozydive-bycer-personalization-and-sandbox]] covers.
- (d) Fit for a mobile casual match-3 + deco game: **medium-high**. Principles 1, 2, 4, 5, 6 translate to tap/drag feedback, variation in repeated effects, interesting failure states (a missed level, an animal "oops"), and eased motion; principle 3 and ownership map to personalisation; "novelty via variants" is cheap for sounds and cat reactions.
