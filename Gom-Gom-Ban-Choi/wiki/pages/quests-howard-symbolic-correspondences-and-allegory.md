# Symbolic correspondences and allegory (Ultima IV, Morrowind, Eternal Darkness)

Source: [[quests-howard-src]], Ch. 1 pp. 29-44; also pp. 36-41 (Gawain design document).

## Core idea
Medieval/Renaissance allegory treats every space, character, object and action as standing for several ideas, interlinked in tables (Sadowski's pentads in *Sir Gawain*; Spenser's dragon = monster + Satan + sin + England's enemies). Howard's design advice: build the game world on a **system of correspondences** that emerges from fun gameplay and is discovered through strategic action, never delivered as a lecture (pp. 29-33).

## Examples cited
- **Ultima IV** (Garriott): eight virtues (from three principles Truth/Love/Courage) mapped to towns, dungeons, colors, stones, mantras, shrines, party companions, icons; stored in eight-bit bytes; whole game as an "Easter egg" of meaning. Garriott avoided preaching, wanting players to consider consequences of their actions (pp. 31-33). Garriott also dropped "kill quests" (slay N beasts for a trophy) in favour of an ethos: live in constant quest to better yourself and the world (p. 35).
- **Eternal Darkness**: runes in three colors map to three Ancients, which map to attack types, in a rock-paper-scissors interplay; the Tome is both lore and interface (journal, inventory, spell-combining) (pp. 33-34, 78-79).
- **Morrowind**: sevens (dreams, visions, riddles, trials, objects) in the main quest (p. 34).
- Rule of thumb: give quests "a specific number of related quests", often collecting parts of one item, so they feel less episodic (p. 33): eight Triforce pieces, twelve episodes/runes, eight virtues, seven trials.
- **Avatar/allegory**: player and avatar are a dual identity; Kelly: MMOs act as "distilled practice versions of real life"; the *Oblivion* "Dreamworld" quest frames games as a training ground testing patience, courage, perception, resolve (pp. 34-35, 56-58).

## Design caveats from Howard
- Correspondences must be integrated into good level design and challenge, not applied mechanically (p. 59).
- Naive, obvious allegory (*Myst*) differs from Spenserian layered allegory (p. 52).
- The Gawain sample design doc (pp. 36-41) lists: spaces, journal updates (written in first person from the player's view), quest items, and scripted challenges, as four lists to draft before building; avoid too many branching dialogue paths (hard to program) and let game rules shape decisions (p. 40).

## Stance and applicability
(a) Assumes lore-rich fantasy RPG/adventure with a symbol-literate audience willing to decode. (b) Literary analysis plus designer interviews (King and Borland on Garriott); player-reception claims are asserted, not measured. (c) Sits at the opposite pole from the "low-stakes, approachable" stance of [[cozydive-bycer-low-stakes-design]] and [[cozydive-bycer-ui-ux-and-approachability]]: cryptic encoded meaning raises cognitive load, which casual cozy players avoid. Compatible with [[concept-dark-game-design-patterns]] only in the sense that "set of N items" completion is also a classic completion-compulsion lever. (d) Fit for casual match-3 + deco: **low**. Cat-and-room theming already carries "meaning"; hidden symbol systems would be over-design for Daily tasks. The one transferable pattern is the fixed-size set (five or eight related milestones that form one picture), see [[quests-howard-quest-items-and-rod-of-many-parts]].
