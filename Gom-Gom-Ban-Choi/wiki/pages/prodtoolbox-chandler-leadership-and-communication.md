# Team leadership, trust, communication, meetings, feedback, conflict

Source: [[prodtoolbox-chandler-src]], Ch12 (pp. 167-182); Ch4.2; Ch7.

## Leadership (Ch12.2)
Leaders show confidence, vision, appreciation, and the courage to make unpopular decisions; defined by actions, not title. Archetypes: charismatic, lead-by-example, motivator. Style depends on person: **instructional** with juniors, **strategic** (goal not method) with seniors. Keep a healthy environment: no bullying even from top performers; be transparent; give autonomy, avoid micromanagement; walk around and listen. Cited: Spaulding *Team Leadership in the Game Industry*; **Game Outcomes Project** (Tozour 2014; five-part study of top-40 things great game teams do - only empirical source in the book).

## Team building (Ch12.3)
A group is directed; a team shares a goal and mutual accountability. Trust: share good and bad news early, honestly, with context (e.g. layoffs), neutralize rumors at a team meeting. Buy-in: explain why a feature fits or not. Motivation: celebrate milestones, say thank you. Introductions, nameplates with role, five-minute personal talks. **Team norms** defined together (point of contact, be constructive, respect time). **Cross-training**: artist tests with QA, engineer imports a level to improve the tool. Sidebars: Taylor - compassionately tell people the truth; Immerman - admit your own mistakes, mention impostor syndrome, balance negative and positive.

## Written communication and meetings (Ch12.4.1-2)
Emails/chat: lead with the action, concise, specifics, mailing lists/channels, sparing "high priority", confirm critical items in person. Meetings: define goal (cancel if none), right attendees, agenda in invite, **30 minutes or less**, time per topic, state goal at start and decisions at end, start/end on time, **do not mix information gathering and decision making**, neutral moderator and minute-taker, action items with owner and deadline, follow-up. Team meeting: project update, new members, Q&A/rumors, fixed time; cancel if nothing; cost of hundreds in a weekly hour.

## Environment, status, information (Ch12.4.3-5)
Seat people by feature (cross-functional) rather than discipline; noise rules, headphones, quiet hours; mix positive personalities in. Status reports tuned to the audience: management weekly/monthly (milestone tracking, risks, key events), team daily (what is ready, next deadline); content: features, marketing/PR, new employees, deadlines, risks, resources. Team website/wiki as single source of truth, must stay current or people revert to asking leads.

## Feedback and conflict (Ch12.4.6-7)
Frequent over annual feedback, timely, examples, behavior not person, questions first. Conflict: wait if emotions high, stick to facts, no "always/never", don't assume intent, never publicly; state facts + project impact, let the person respond, propose remedy and a path to reopen (script example: a designer kept working on a cut feature). **Consensus means acceptance, not agreement**. Sidebar (Smith): empathy for players, developers and publisher.

## Stance and applicability
(a) Producer or lead managing 10+ people in an office, with email as the main medium; US management-culture vocabulary (norms, nameplates, team websites).
(b) Practitioner opinion and general management literature; one empirical source cited but not summarized in the book's text.
(c) Qualifies: Scrum/agile replaces status reports with stand-ups, burn-downs and retrospectives; Chandler keeps both. Strongly opposed to crunch-style management (she says never schedule overtime, Ch7.6) - compare industry crunch cultures. For live-ops teams the metrics dashboard serves as status; see [[concept-analytics-and-kpis]]. Cross-training echoes designer-programmer pairing in small teams.
(d) Fit: medium. Meeting rules, decision vs information separation, and conflict script are universally portable; the status-report and wiki advice also supports the existing repo wiki practice (`wiki/` as single source of truth; CLAUDE.md as living rules). Designer-only: medium - designers who lead design need trust-building, written communication and feedback skills, but not budget or staffing.
