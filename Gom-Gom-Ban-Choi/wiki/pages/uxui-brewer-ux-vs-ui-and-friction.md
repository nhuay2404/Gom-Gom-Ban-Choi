# UX vs UI, and games as "balanced friction"

Source: [[uxui-brewer-src]], Ch. 1 (pp. 1-4), Ch. 4 intro (pp. 28-32), Ch. 5 "UX Reality" (pp. 75-76), Ch. 10 (Jordan DeVries p. 167, Ian Plater p. 156).

## Claims
- UX = whole experience from first contact onward; UI = the visual interaction points that make it possible. Car analogy: UI is wheels/seats/buttons, logic is engine, UX includes why the car exists and how it feels to drive (p. 56). They are not interchangeable but "intertwined"; the author dismisses online gatekeeping about definitions (pp. 14, 98, 171).
- Short history: Engelbart GUI, Norman 1988 (user-centred design, affordances, feedback; Norman coined "user experience"), Tennis for Two vs Pong (is the paddle UI? the maze?). In games UX used to arise passively from programmers/artists; specialists only appeared with bigger budgets and smartphones (pp. 1-3).
- Core distinction: apps/web remove friction (order the pizza fast); games need friction as challenge, so game UX adds and removes friction to hit design intent (pp. 4, 28, 52). "A game with no friction is effectively a screensaver" (DeVries, p. 76/167). Apps funnel to purchase; games want the player digging out of a hole (p. 167).
- Seven UX qualities (honeycomb): useful, usable, discoverable, credible, desirable, accessible, valuable (pp. 29-31). Discoverable example: store price, balance and checkout button must be findable (p. 29-30). Credible: players must feel time and money are well spent. Desirable: games are luxury goods; desirability drives word of mouth and cosmetic pricing (p. 30).
- "It's not about you": preference belongs to style/tone/story, not usability; avoid the "I like..." director (p. 31-32). Ford/Jobs quotes: users cannot specify the future, but you still must understand them. Echoed by McConnell: "everyone is a user, but not everyone is THE user" (p. 156).
- Some games succeed with poor UX (franchise, strong core fun, Among Us patched later), so UX is insurance and retention, not a sales driver; the ideal UX is not noticed (pp. 28-29, 171).
- Reality check: game design is king and trumps research; stakeholders resist; UX is "primarily about adaptation", not an exact science; making players feel is also the goal, not only sales (pp. 75-76).
- Do not redesign for its own sake; players like change only when something is broken or requested; keep changes small (p. 71).

## Reusable takeaways
- Use the honeycomb as a quick review checklist for each screen (shop, Deco panel, level map).
- Decide per surface whether friction is intentional: the match-3 board is deliberate friction; menus, shop, claim flows are not.

## Stance and applicability
- (a) AAA narrative/console/PC studio view (Supermassive, Respawn, Bungie interviewees); platform mobile mentioned only for touch size. No F2P or monetisation framing.
- (b) Practitioner opinion plus textbook UX theory (Norman, honeycomb); no data. Claim "bad UX games still sell" is anecdotal.
- (c) Consistent with [[prodtoolbox-chandler-ux-process]] and [[mobiledesign-scolastici-four-keys-to-fun]]; qualifies [[src-gd-fresher-f2p-monetization-training]] / [[concept-offers-and-merchandising]], which treat shop UI as a sales funnel, whereas Brewer calls the app-style conversion funnel the wrong model for games (though he concedes a store must be discoverable and credible).
- (d) Fit: medium-high. The friction framing and honeycomb are directly usable for G1 review; but nothing on retention/IAP UX, so it is a vocabulary source, not a design source for the shop.
