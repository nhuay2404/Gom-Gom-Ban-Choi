# Meaning in quests: initiation, narrative, theme, and the quest spectrum

Source: [[quests-howard-src]], Introduction pp. 19-28; Ch. 1 pp. 29-30.

## Spectrum of quests (p. 22-24)
Games sit on a line from narrative-heavy (point-and-click adventure) to action-heavy (MMO: "a profusion of quests but very little narrative, resulting in constant action that has little meaning"). Tasks that are "most truly quests" sit in the middle: single-player action-adventure and RPG (*Oblivion*, *Dreamfall*, *Eternal Darkness*). Quests can exist in any goal-oriented game (arcade, sports, flight sim) but only as marginal cases; full-criteria quests appear in adventure, action-adventure, RPG, FPS, survival horror and MMO.

In MMOs, Howard argues, "meaning" collapses to personal achievement (a level-60 character in expensive armor); Kaplan (WoW lead quest designer) says quests exist to push players to explore the world, with "breadcrumb quests" (p. 47).

## Three types of meaning (pp. 25-28)
1. **Impact/achievement ("initiation")**: powering up and learning lore; changing the world, the political/moral balance, relationships with companions. Leveling is the secular form of ritual initiation (guild ranks novice/apprentice/journeyman/expert/master; mystery religions; Freemasonry degrees). "The impetus behind quests is achievement" (p. 27-28).
2. **Narrative**: backstory that gives urgency (why am I doing this?) and a payoff that explains what happened or will happen. The pull of "what happened / what next" can motivate heavy time investment (p. 28).
3. **Thematic/expressive**: ideas symbolically encoded in landscape, objects, challenges and enacted by the player (good vs evil as battles; puzzles that hide encoded meaning). See [[quests-howard-symbolic-correspondences-and-allegory]].

Test of meaningfulness from the player's side: is the quest "a worthwhile expenditure of time and effort rather than an unrewarding chore?" (pp. 25-26).

## Oblivion Mage's Guild example (pp. 24-25)
First seven guild quests are trivial fetch/kill/crawl tasks with small local stakes; the quest-giver then says the player is ready for "more meaningful tasks". Later quests are mechanically the same (fetch, kill) but reveal and alter a large conflict (necromancy plot) and grant ranks. Moral: "exciting gameplay is a necessary but not a sufficient component of a meaningful and enjoyable quest"; the *same quest types* gain value by context, ranks and consequence.

## Quest design starts from the big picture (p. 29)
Before designing, ask: why is this quest important to the player, what satisfaction beyond killing and loot, and which overarching picture of meaning and action does it serve?

## Stance and applicability
(a) RPG/adventure design for audiences that already want an "epic experience"; MMO grind is treated as the weak end of the spectrum. (b) Theory plus designer interviews (Kaplan, Rolston) and critic opinion; no retention or engagement data. (c) Partly echoes [[concept-reward-design-and-psychology]] (rewards beyond fungible currency) and Barton's "better rewards than XP and gold"; conflicts mildly with the daily-task design in [[concept-spreadsheet-modeling]] and [[council-topic-verdicts]], where short repeatable tasks are accepted, because Howard treats repetitive proliferating tasks as low-meaning. Aligns with the anti-grind warnings in [[concept-dark-game-design-patterns]]. (d) Fit for casual match-3 + deco: **low-medium**. Useful reading for milestone Quests: tie each milestone to a visible consequence (a room unlocks, a cat reacts, a story beat) rather than a bare number, and let achievement/initiation (ranks, titles, a deco "level") be the primary meaning layer. Daily tasks: little to take; keep them short and low-stakes by design.
