# Real-time timers, daily play and the "conditioning" remark (Bycer)

Source: [[cozydive-bycer-src]] ch. 3.1 (p. 12), 3.5 (p. 35), 5.2 (p. 76), 5.5 (p. 97), 5.4. Thin material, collected here because it bears on our energy/timer/daily-reward questions.

## What he actually says
- **Browser games (3.1)**: popularised by real-world timers (minutes, hours, days): log in for a few minutes, set things running, return when timers finish. Simple gameplay let them extend almost indefinitely with new goals; people played daily for months or years; many centred on guild vs guild competition despite the casual look. Descriptive, not judged.
- **Animal Crossing (3.5)**: the calendar tied to the real world "provided a structure and reward for consistent playing as opposed to lengthy plays". Tangent (opinion): this incentive to turn a game into a daily task "would be heavily adopted by the free-to-play and mobile market in the 2010s and can be abused" by conditioning players to play every day.
- **Two play archetypes (3.5)**: short story games, and long-term games not meant for extended sessions; players can spend hours or a few minutes then return tomorrow.
- **Stardew day structure (3.4)**: shops closed some days, festivals every few weeks, seasons: a moving world that rewards presence without loss.
- **Countdown timers (5.2)**: avoid those that force fast decisions; clearly communicated time-limited goals are acceptable.
- **Late-2000s casual (5.5)**: easy start then difficulty spikes and pain points to motivate spending - the source of the "casual" stigma.
- **Idle design (5.4)**: progress by automation; players watch passively; he endorses cozy idle (quarter-view) and short-form idle (3-5 hours), and warns against games requiring daily play for weeks.
- No discussion of energy refill pricing, login streaks, FOMO limited-time events, battle passes or rewarded ads.

## Stance and applicability
(a) Premium cozy games; real-time mechanics are discussed only as history and as a warning against F2P habits. (b) One-line opinion with Animal Crossing as the documented example; no evidence on abuse rates. (c) Directly qualifies [[concept-pacing-and-content-control]] (energy as session control and habit) and [[concept-monetization-strategies]]; agrees with [[concept-dark-game-design-patterns]] on appointment mechanics (daily conditioning) and with [[src-zagal-dark-patterns-in-game-design]]; it disagrees with the GD Fresher deck ([[src-gd-fresher-f2p-monetization-training]]) only by implication, since that deck frames daily rewards and energy as retention tools. The calendar-reward model he credits (presence rewarded, absence not punished) is not the same as streak loss. (d) Fit: medium. Safe takeaways: reward return without punishing absence (no streak reset, no timed forfeits), keep timers optional or informational in the deco meta, avoid countdowns on creative decisions. Energy/lives in a match-3 remain a business decision; Bycer gives the cozy-brand risk, not a ban. See also [[concept-hybrid-models-and-ethics]].
