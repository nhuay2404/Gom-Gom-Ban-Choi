# Source: Dark Patterns in the Design of Games

Zagal, Björk, Lewis — Foundations of Digital Games (FDG) 2013, paper 06. 6-page academic paper.
URL: http://www.fdg2013.org/program/papers/paper06_zagal_etal.pdf (downloaded and read in full; not copied into `raw/`).

## Thesis
Designers are usually called advocates for the player, but a creator's interests can conflict with players'. The authors name **Dark Game Design Patterns**: abstracted gameplay design elements used **intentionally** to cause negative experiences that go **against the player's best interests** and **without their consent** (final definition, p. 6). It is prescriptive ("arguably should not be done"), unlike value-neutral design patterns.

Details, the pattern catalogue and guiding questions: [[concept-dark-game-design-patterns]].

## Key points
- **Anti-pattern vs dark pattern**: bad design can come from ignorance or lack of time; a dark pattern must be purposeful ("designing for bad", not "bad design").
- **Consent matters more than whether the experience is negative**: hard, painful or deceptive games are fine when players knowingly accept them (Painstation; Eternal Darkness fake "crash"; rubber-band AI that serves the player's enjoyment).
- Darkness depends on context, implementation, audience, and whether the pattern **supports** the player's chosen play style.
- **Disclosure principle** (Berdichevsky and Neuenschwander): creators should disclose motivations, methods and intended outcomes. Players' *manipulation literacy* grows over time, so a pattern known to players is less dark; the authors warn of "poisoning the well" (a tragedy of the commons) if the industry keeps using manipulation.
- Gambling is **not** counted as a dark pattern because players knowingly participate (so lootbox disclosure laws in [[concept-monetization-strategies]] are about making that knowledge real).
