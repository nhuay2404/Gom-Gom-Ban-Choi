# Metaphor: representation, treatment and harmony of expectations

Source: [[gamefeel-swink-src]], Ch. 5 (pp. 96-98), Ch. 10 (pp. 171-178), Ch. 17 harmony (pp. 306-308), Ch. 19 (pp. 340-342).

## Claims
- Metaphor has two parts: **representation** (what the object is: a car, a cat, a plumber) and **treatment** (how the art/sound is executed: realistic, iconic, abstract). Together they prime the player with expectations about how the thing should behave, sound and interact (p. 171-172). Same code, different avatar (a car vs a running fat man) gives a different feel (p. 97). Players say "this doesn't feel like a horse" with no horse experience.
- **Uncanny valley of game feel**: the closer the treatment is to photoreal, the stricter the expectations; near-misses (Doom 3's cap-gun shots, identical dangling zombies; Project Gotham's waterslide car collisions) hurt more than abstraction would. Cartoon treatment gives leeway (Mario Kart karts bump with no dissonance; heavier karts knock lighter ones, more sophisticated than PGR3) (pp. 172-175).
- Scott McCloud triangle: realistic, iconic, abstract; a game's behaviour, sound and effects must sit at the same level ("consistency of abstraction"); iconic games tolerate jelly collisions if volume is preserved (animation principle) (pp. 175-177, 306-307).
- Expectation effects are like a wine tasting: labels change perceived quality, so be careful what you promise (p. 177).
- Surreal treatments tolerate surreal controls (Mario flies like a flea but no wind-up is expected). A recognisable iconic body can signal state changes (ground vs air) (pp. 225-226).
- Photorealism sets up failure ("games that strive for real-world fidelity set themselves up to fail"); future should explore iconic and abstract metaphors and role shifting ("what if you played as the castle / the weather / a fluid") (pp. 340-342).
- Harmony: each element must support one perceived physical reality; motion is the hardest part (running into a wall and staying animated breaks the illusion; Gears of War turned wall-pressing into the cover mechanic) (pp. 307-308).

## Stance and applicability
- (a) Real-time avatar games, mostly console; considers realism as the chief hazard.
- (b) Essayistic and case-based (Mario Kart Wii vs Project Gotham 3 comparison); no data.
- (c) Supports the toon/iconic look already chosen (CLAUDE.md toon rules) and agrees with [[mobiledesign-scolastici-graphics-and-character-readability]] (readability over fidelity) and [[cozydive-bycer-defining-cozy-wholesome-casual]] (aesthetic as promise). Adds a rule those sources lack: behaviour, sound, UI motion must match the level of abstraction promised by the art.
- (d) Fit for a mobile casual match-3 + deco game: **medium-high**. Cats in a toon world set cat expectations (curious, soft, nap, stretch); motion and sound should be cat-like and cartoony, and the cat-face table (calm, cute, happy, sleepy, grumble, annoyed) is an application of treatment-consistent feedback. Fit is limited to checking mismatches: nothing here tells how to design a match-3 board.
