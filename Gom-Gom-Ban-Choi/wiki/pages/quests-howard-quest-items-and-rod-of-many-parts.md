# Quest items, rewards-as-objects and the "rod of many parts"

Source: [[quests-howard-src]], Ch. 4, pp. 77-99.

## Item tiers (p. 77)
1. **Clutter**: minor items giving realism, no quest impact.
2. **Functional**: weapons, armor, potions; usually *rewards* of quests or loot.
3. **Quest/plot items**: essential to the backstory and often powerful. A "+10 sword with a long backstory" is only a mediocre quest item.

## What makes a good quest item (pp. 80-86)
Qualities: **symbolism**, **function within gameplay**, **value in the game economy**, **distinctive appearance**. Best items are sparse, iconic, mysterious (fairy-tale style): *Morrowind*'s star-and-crescent mark was the desktop icon; Ultima's virtue icons; the Codex of Ultimate Wisdom appears *after* the player has earned eight stones, so the player constructs its meaning. *Planescape: Torment* tattoos record every quest, even a minor matchmaking side quest, as a glyph on the character with a stat bonus, reflecting how the player resolved it: "quest history as visible, functional reward" (pp. 80-82).
- Quest items sit in an in-between space: bracketed out of normal inventory (cannot be sold or discarded) yet extremely powerful at key moments (pp. 88-89).
- **Lore** enhances item interest and replay (item-description paragraphs). Balance lore with economy (Dwemer artifacts: valuable but heavy, so transport becomes a puzzle) (p. 85-86).
- Propp's object-transfer functions as gameplay verbs: buy, sell, fetch after an NPC's indication, trade, find hidden, loot (pp. 83-84). Hitchcock's "McGuffin": the item matters as a motivator, not in itself (p. 84). Strategic effort to acquire an item gives pride in it (p. 84).

## The rod of many parts (pp. 86-88)
Rolston's maxim "the greatest story is the rod of eight parts" and Barwood's formulation: give the story a physical form (corporealize) and then break it into pieces (atomize) and scatter them to drive exploration and challenges. Lineage: 1982 D&D module "Dwarvenkind: Quest for the Rod of Seven Parts" (each part a Latin word of a sentence, each a usable power), *Bard's Tale II* (7 Destiny Wand parts), *Zelda* (8 Triforce pieces, medallions), *Arena* (Staff of Chaos, 8 parts). Meaning is emergent: the player slowly grasps what the pieces add up to. Reassembled/partial sets can be used piece by piece or fused into a powerful whole (p. 87).

## Item creation advice (pp. 90-99)
Mostly toolset tutorials (Item Wizard, hakpaks, Gmax). Principles: items should be symbolic, useful in gameplay, revealing of the underlying narrative; model in 3D when possible rather than a generic icon (p. 94). Design resources: symbol dictionaries, tarot, heraldry, Jung, secret alphabets/runes (p. 90).

## Stance and applicability
(a) Fantasy RPG with inventory, equipment and rare artifacts; "reward" is an object the player equips. (b) Design analysis with historical lineage; no measured effects. (c) Complements [[concept-reward-design-and-psychology]]: Howard's rewards are *meaningful, collected, persistent* items, consistent with endowment effect/collection advice, and with temporary-collection patterns in [[liveops-vasiuk-event-canvas-and-features]]. The "complete the set to unlock the big thing" mechanism is also a completion-compulsion lever that [[concept-dark-game-design-patterns]] flags; for us it should be non-expiring. Reward-as-visible-record (tattoo) parallels our deco items placed in a room. (d) Fit for casual match-3 + deco: **medium**. The rod-of-parts structure maps cleanly onto **milestone Quests**: one goal broken into N visible parts (e.g. five pieces of a cat tree) with partial progress shown, pieces earned over time, and the whole as the payoff. It is not applicable to Daily tasks (five easy coin tasks have no object story); keep Daily rewards as plain coins per our design.
